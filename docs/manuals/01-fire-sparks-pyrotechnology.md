# Operational Manual 01: Fire, Sparks, and Pyrotechnology

> "To make fire without matches or modern lighters, you must understand the physics of pyrophoric oxidation, friction kinetics, and the preparation of pyrolyzed tinder."

---

## 1. Percussion Sparks: Striking Two Stones

You cannot make reliable fire by hitting any two random river stones together. Quartz on quartz produces triboluminescence (a brief cold glow beneath the surface), not hot sparks.

### The Required Minerals
To produce a glowing ember, you must strike **Iron Pyrite ($\text{FeS}_2$)** or **Marcasite** against a hard, sharp **Flint/Chert** nodule (or hardened high-carbon steel file).

```
   [ Flint Nodule with Sharp 60° Ridge ] (Held in Left Hand with Char-cloth)
                 ^
                 | (Glancing blow at 45° angle)
   [ Iron Pyrite / Marcasite Nodule ]   (Held in Right Hand)
                 |
                 v
   Sparks Shaved Downward onto Char-cloth Ember Collector
```

### Physical Principle: Pyrophoric Friction
- Flint has a Mohs hardness of $7.0$; pyrite has a hardness of $6.0\text{--}6.5$.
- When the sharp flint edge scrapes across the crystalline pyrite at high speed ($\approx 10\text{ m/s}$), the flint shaves off microscopic flakes of metallic iron and sulfur.
- The kinetic friction of the scrape heats these microscopic fragments above $450^\circ\text{C}$.
- At this elevated temperature, the high surface-area-to-mass ratio of the iron flake causes it to spontaneously combust in atmospheric oxygen ($\text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + \Delta H$), creating a shower of glowing sparks burning at over **$800^\circ\text{C}\text{ to } 1000^\circ\text{C}$**.

## 2. Preparing the Tinder: Char-Cloth and Amadou

A spark from pyrite lasts only a fraction of a second. It will bounce off raw wood or dry grass without catching. It *must* land on a pre-pyrolyzed low-ignition material.

### Recipe: How to Make Char-Cloth
1. **Material**: Pure woven plant cellulose: old linen cloth, cotton rag, or dry bast fiber. Synthetic fibers or wool will melt into plastic slag and fail.
2. **The Char-Tin**: Take a small clay pot with a tight-fitting clay lid (or a metal tin). Punch a single $1\text{ mm}$ pinhole in the top to vent volatile gases.
3. **Pyrolysis**: Pack the cloth inside loosely. Place the sealed tin directly onto red-hot fireplace coals.
4. **Combustion Control**: Dense white smoke and combustible wood-gas vapors will jet out of the pinhole. After 10–15 minutes, when the gas jet stops smoking and flames die out, immediately remove the tin from the fire using tongs.
5. **Cooling in Absence of Oxygen**: **DO NOT OPEN THE TIN WHILE HOT.** If fresh oxygen enters while the char is above its auto-ignition temperature, the contents will burst into ash. Allow to cool completely to ambient temperature.
6. **Result**: The cloth emerges velvety black, brittle, and fully carbonized (nearly pure porous amorphous carbon). The slightest contact with an $800^\circ\text{C}$ spark will ignite a self-propagating glowing red ember.

### The Natural Alternative: Amadou Fungus
- Harvest the bracket fungus *Fomes fomentarius* (horse hoof fungus) from dead hardwood trees.
- Slice away the hard woody crust. Extract the soft, suede-like middle fungal layer (the trama).
- Boil the suede strips in water mixed with wood ash lye (potassium carbonate) for 12 hours to dissolve resins and separate micro-fibers.
- Pound with a smooth wooden mallet and dry in the sun. Yields a fluffy tinder felt that catches cold pyrite sparks instantly.

## 3. Igniting the Flame: Step-by-Step Procedure

```
[ Step 1: Strike Spark ] ===> [ Step 2: Catch on Char-cloth ] ===> [ Step 3: Tinder Nest ] ===> [ Step 4: Open Flame ]
  Pyrite strikes flint          Ember glows & spreads red           Blow smoothly into tinder     Dry twigs catch fire
```

1. Hold the sharp flint nodule in your left hand, placing a square of char-cloth directly on top of the flint, its edge aligned 1 mm behind the sharp striking ridge.
2. Strike the pyrite nodule down across the flint ridge in a sharp, glancing blow at a $45^\circ$ angle, as if trying to shave a thin layer off the pyrite.
3. Sparks shave off and land directly on the carbonized char-cloth.
4. When a spark lands, a faint red ring begins glowing and expanding across the black fabric.
5. Place the glowing char-cloth inside a loose "bird's nest" of bone-dry tinder (fine shredded cedar/juniper bark, crushed dried grass, or flax tow).
6. Fold the tinder nest loosely around the ember, hold it at eye level, and blow with long, steady, gentle breaths.
7. Smoke thickens, the ember turns blinding orange, and with a sudden whoosh, the tinder bursts into an open yellow flame.
8. Touch the flame to dry, pencil-lead-thin kindling sticks arranged in a teepee, gradually adding finger-thick branches, then wrist-thick dry hardwood logs.

## 4. Forced-Draft Air Supply: The Double-Acting Pot Bellows

An open wood fire peaks at $700^\circ\text{C}\text{ to } 800^\circ\text{C}$—insufficient to melt copper ($1085^\circ\text{C}$) or smelt iron ($1200^\circ\text{C}+$). You must pump forced air into charcoal.

```
       [ Operator's Hand/Foot Operating Plunger ]
                         |
           +-------------v-------------+
           | Flexible Goat-skin Cover  |
           +---------------------------+
           | Ceramic Pot Bellows Body  |  <--- 40 cm diameter, 30 cm deep clay bowl
           +-------------+-------------+
                         |
                         v (Reed Flapper Check Valve)
           +-------------+-------------+
           | Ceramic Tuyere Tube       |  <--- 4 cm bore, pointing into charcoal core
           +---------------------------+
                         |
                         v
             [ White-Hot Smelting Fire: 1200°C+ ]
```

1. **Clay Pots**: Throw two flat-bottomed earthenware pots ($40\text{ cm}$ diameter, $30\text{ cm}$ depth).
2. **Leather Diaphragm**: Stretch supple, oiled goat or sheep skin over the open top rim, tied air-tight with linen cord. Attach a wooden stick to the center of each skin as a handle.
3. **Flapper Intake Valve**: Cut a $4\text{ cm}$ hole in the center of the leather skin. Fasten a flap of stiff leather beneath the hole inside the pot. When the operator pulls up, the flap drops open, drawing in air; when pushed down, internal air pressure slams the flap shut, forcing all air out through the side tube.
4. **Tuyere**: Mold a heat-resistant ceramic pipe (alluvial clay + $40\%$ sand grog) from the pot base into the furnace wall.
5. **Continuous Operation**: By pumping the left and right bellows alternately with feet or hands, the operator provides a continuous, pulse-free stream of oxygen into the heart of the fuel, driving local temperatures past **$1300^\circ\text{C}$**.
