# Metallurgy: From Copper Prills to Superalloys

> "A stone tool breaks upon impact; a copper tool bends and can be hammered straight; a bronze tool holds its edge; a steel tool cuts the others. Metallurgy is the physical mastery of the crystal lattice."

---

## 1. The Pyrotechnic Hierarchy

Extracting metals from ores requires chemical reduction (removing oxygen and sulfur) under controlled temperatures and reducing atmospheres:

| Metal | Mineral Form | Smelting Temp (°C) | Atmosphere Required | Flux Used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Copper (Cu)** | Malachite $\text{Cu}_2\text{CO}_3(\text{OH})_2$ | 1085 | Reducing ($CO$) | Hematite or Quartz sand |
| **Lead (Pb)** | Galena $\text{PbS}$ | 327 (roasting + smelt) | Oxidizing roast, then reducing | Iron oxide |
| **Tin (Sn)** | Cassiterite $\text{SnO}_2$ | 1150 | Strongly reducing ($CO$) | Limestone |
| **Iron (Fe)** | Hematite $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ | 1100–1250 (bloom) / 1538 (liquid) | Highly reducing ($CO$) | Limestone / Fayalite slag |
| **Steel (Fe-C)** | Carburized wrought iron | 900 (diffusion) / 1400 (melt) | Controlled carbon potential | Borax / Glass seal |
| **Aluminum (Al)** | Bauxite $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | 950 (electrolytic) | Molten cryolite salt bath | Cryolite $\text{Na}_3\text{AlF}_6$ |

## 2. Copper Smelting

### Thermodynamic Principle
In a charcoal fire blown with air, carbon combusts to carbon dioxide, which in excess incandescent charcoal forms carbon monoxide (the Boudouard equilibrium):
$$\text{C} + \text{CO}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{CO}$$

Carbon monoxide reduces copper oxide (derived from calcined malachite):
$$\text{Cu}_2\text{CO}_3(\text{OH})_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{CuO} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\text{CuO} + \text{CO} \to \text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2$$

Molten copper has a density of $8.96\text{ g/cm}^3$, causing it to sink beneath the lighter silicate slag ($d \approx 3.0\text{ g/cm}^3$).

### Crucible Smelting Protocol
1. **Refractory Ceramic Crucible**: Mix alluvial clay with 30% crushed quartz grog and chopped straw. Line interior with bone ash or charcoal wash to prevent copper sticking.
2. **Charge**: Layer crushed malachite ore (pea-sized, 5–10 mm) interleaved with crushed hardwood charcoal.
3. **Air Blast**: Insert refractory ceramic tuyere (nozzle) connected to foot-operated pot bellows or double-acting bag bellows. Pump continuously for 90–120 minutes.
4. **Coalescence**: Copper prills coalesce at the crucible base. Tap slag from the surface and allow the copper button to cool, or pour into carved sandstone ingot molds.

## 3. Bronze: The Tin-Copper Alloy

Pure copper is ductile and work-hardens quickly, but its as-cast hardness is low ($\approx 50\text{ HV}$). Alloying copper with 10–12 wt% tin fundamentally alters the lattice:

- **Solid Solution & Intermetallics**: Tin atoms (atomic radius 140 pm) substitute for copper atoms (radius 128 pm) in the face-centered cubic (FCC) copper lattice. This creates substantial lattice strain, blocking dislocation movement (solid solution strengthening).
- **Hardness**: 10% tin-bronze cast has a hardness of $\approx 100\text{ HV}$; work-hammering raises it to $> 220\text{ HV}$—rivaling mild carbon steel.
- **Melting Point Depression**: Tin lowers the melting point of copper from $1085^\circ\text{C}$ down to $\approx 950^\circ\text{C}$, lowering fuel demand and dramatically improving fluidity for thin-walled castings.

### Lost-Wax (Investment) Casting
1. Sculpt the component in beeswax (cire perdue), including sprue and riser channels.
2. Coat wax with multiple layers of fine clay slip mixed with cow dung fiber.
3. Bake mold in a low fire: wax melts and drains out, firing the ceramic hollow core.
4. Pour molten bronze at $1050^\circ\text{C}$ into the hot preheated mold. Break ceramic mold once cooled.

## 4. Bloomery Iron and the Steel Transformation

Iron cannot be melted in early furnaces because its melting point ($1538^\circ\text{C}$) exceeds primitive bellows capability. Instead, early metallurgists performed **solid-state direct reduction**:

### The Bloomery Furnace
- A vertical clay shaft (1.5 m tall, 0.4 m inner diameter) charged with alternating layers of charcoal and high-grade hematite or bog iron ore.
- Air blown at the base reduces $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ to solid metallic iron sponge while gangue silica reacts with iron oxide to form liquid fayalite slag:
$$2\text{FeO} + \text{SiO}_2 \to \text{Fe}_2\text{SiO}_4 \quad (\text{liquid at } 1150^\circ\text{C})$$
- The molten slag is tapped through a lower hole. The spongy solid mass of iron crystals (the bloom) is extracted through an opening in the furnace wall.

### Consolidation and Carburizing
1. **Hammer Welding**: Reheat the red-hot ($1100^\circ\text{C}$) bloom on an anvil and hammer vigorously. Liquid slag inclusions squirt out, consolidating porous iron crystals into solid wrought iron ($< 0.05\%\text{ C}$).
2. **Pack Carburization (Case Hardening)**: Pack wrought iron blades in sealed clay canisters filled with charcoal dust, crushed animal bone, and leather scraps. Heat at $950^\circ\text{C}$ for 12 hours. Carbon atoms diffuse into the surface, forming high-carbon steel ($0.7\text{--}1.0\%\text{ C}$).
3. **Quenching**: Heat steel to cherry red ($800^\circ\text{C}$, fully transforming into Face-Centered Cubic **Austenite**). Plunge rapidly into cold water or brine. The rapid cooling traps carbon atoms inside the lattice before they can precipitate out, forcing the lattice into a strained Body-Centered Tetragonal structure: **Martensite** ($\approx 65\text{ HRC}$, extremely hard but glass-brittle).
4. **Tempering**: Gently reheat quenched blade to $220\text{--}280^\circ\text{C}$ (judged by straw-to-blue oxidation colors on polished metal) and air cool. Martensite relaxes slightly into tempered martensite, relieving internal stresses and yielding a springy, durable cutting edge.

## 5. Industrial Mass Steelmaking

### Blast Furnace Coke Smelting (Abraham Darby, 1709)
Replacing charcoal with devolatilized coal (metallurgical coke) allows giant blast furnaces (10–30 meters tall) to support enormous structural ore weights without crushing the charge, producing liquid high-carbon **pig iron** ($4\%\text{ C}$, melting point $1150^\circ\text{C}$).

### Bessemer Pneumatic Decarburization (Henry Bessemer, 1856)
- Pour 10 tons of molten pig iron into a pear-shaped converter lined with silica refractory bricks.
- Blast cold compressed air through bottom tuyeres at 1.5 bar.
- The oxidation of silicon, manganese, and carbon is intensely exothermic:
$$\text{Si} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{SiO}_2 + \Delta H$$
$$2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{CO} \uparrow + \Delta H$$
- The molten metal temperature jumps autonomously from $1200^\circ\text{C}$ to $> 1600^\circ\text{C}$ without burning a single piece of external fuel!
- After 15 minutes of violent flame, carbon drops to $< 0.05\%$. Add precise doses of ferromanganese (spiegeleisen) to deoxidize the bath and set the carbon content to exactly $0.25\%$ (structural mild steel).

## 6. Modern Aerospace and Frontier Metallurgy

1. **Hall-Héroult Electrolytic Aluminum (1886)**: Dissolve pure calcined alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) in molten cryolite ($\text{Na}_3\text{AlF}_6$) at $950^\circ\text{C}$. Pass 50,000–300,000 Amperes through carbon anodes. Pure aluminum sinks to the cathode bottom. Unlocks lightweight structural aerospace airframes.
2. **Kroll Titanium Process (1940)**: Chlorinate rutile/ilmenite to titanium tetrachloride ($\text{TiCl}_4$). Reduce in argon atmosphere with molten magnesium at $850^\circ\text{C}$ to yield titanium sponge. Vacuum arc remelt with aluminum and vanadium into **Ti-6Al-4V** for jet engine turbine discs and rocket cryogenic tanks.
3. **Nickel-Based Superalloys (Inconel 718 / CMSX-4)**: Vacuum induction melting single-crystal turbine blades containing nickel, chromium, cobalt, molybdenum, and rhenium. Coherent precipitate strengthening by gamma-prime phase $\text{Ni}_3(\text{Al, Ti})$ prevents high-temperature dislocation creep up to $1100^\circ\text{C}$ inside rocket turbopumps.
