# R000 — Raw Earth Resources (Terminal Nodes)

These are the **absolute primitives** — materials taken directly from nature with no prior processing.
Each entry is a **terminal node**: the recursive decomposition chain ends here.

## R000-A · Flint / Chert

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Microcrystalline silica (SiO₂). Black, grey, or brown nodules.
  Conchoidal fracture — breaks with razor-sharp curved edges.
  Mohs hardness: 7.0 (scratches glass, scratches iron pyrite).

WHERE TO FIND IT (Egypt, 2975 BCE):
  • Nile Valley flint beds — alluvial gravel, any Nile bank
  • Sinai plateau — surface nodules, abundant (29–30°N 33–34°E)
  • Kharga Oasis — flint-rich limestone formations (25.4°N 30.6°E)
  • Eastern Desert plateau edges

HOW TO IDENTIFY IN FIELD:
  1. Pick up any dark, glassy-looking stone.
  2. Strike it with another hard stone — does it ring sharply and
     produce a conchoidal (shell-curved) chip? → Flint.
  3. Does it scratch glass or feldspar? → Confirmed ≥ Mohs 7.

HOW TO COLLECT:
  • Surface-collect nodules from river gravel bars.
    No tools required. 1 person: 50 kg/day.
  • For high-quality: mine flint bands in chalk/limestone cliffs
    using wooden wedges and antler picks.
    Yield: 100 kg/worker/day in a good seam.

USEFUL SIZES:
  • Fire-striking: fist-sized (10×8×5 cm), ~400g
  • Knapping blanks: palm-sized, tabular, 150–300g
  • Scraper/chisel: small, 50–100g

REQUIRED EFFORT: 1 person × 1 hour → 10 kg suitable nodules
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-B · Iron Pyrite / Marcasite

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  FeS₂. Brassy-yellow, cubic or radial crystals.
  Mohs hardness: 6.0–6.5.
  Produces HOT sparks when struck against flint (Mohs 7 > 6.5 →
  flint shaves microscopic iron particles; air friction ignites them).

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  • Eastern Desert quartz veins (28–26°N, 33–32°E)
  • Sinai copper belt — associated with chalcopyrite
  • Nile gravel (eroded from Eastern Desert highlands)
  • Look for: heavy, brassy-yellow nodules in quartz veins

HOW TO IDENTIFY:
  1. Colour: brass-yellow (not gold — gold is heavier, 19.3 g/cm³
     vs pyrite 5.0 g/cm³; pyrite feels lighter per volume).
  2. Strike with iron or flint → bright white-yellow sparks.
  3. Scratch test: scratches glass (Mohs 6.5) but flint scratches it.

REQUIRED EFFORT: 1 person × 2 hours → 1 kg pyrite nodules
  (rarer than flint; worth carrying a 500g nodule as permanent tool)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-C · Malachite Ore (Copper Carbonate)

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Cu₂(CO₃)(OH)₂. Bright green, banded, waxy luster.
  Mohs: 3.5–4. Density: 3.9 g/cm³. Fizzes with vinegar.
  Cu content in pure mineral: 57.4%.
  Real field ore (with gangue): 20–40% Cu by weight.

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  PRIMARY SOURCES (all accessible from Egypt):
  • Wadi Dara, Eastern Desert — 27.9°N 33.2°E
    Distance from Memphis: 350 km (12-day camel caravan)
    Surface outcrops, green staining on limestone cliffs.
    Extraction rate: 100 kg ore/worker/day (surface collection)
  • Serabit el-Khadim, Sinai — 29.1°N 33.5°E
    Distance from Memphis: 250 km (8-day journey via Sinai road)
    Ancient Egyptian mining district (mining records from 2600 BCE+)
  • Timna Valley, Negev — 29.8°N 34.9°E
    Distance: 400 km. Rich in native copper + malachite.
  • Wadi Hammamat — 25.9°N 34.0°E
    With other Eastern Desert ores.

HOW TO IDENTIFY:
  1. Bright green colour on rock face (blue = azurite, also copper).
  2. Fizzes with a drop of vinegar (dilute acetic acid). Malachite
     dissolves, releasing CO₂ bubbles.
  3. Streak test: pale green on unglazed porcelain (use a smooth
     limestone chip as streak plate).
  4. Density: noticeably heavy for size (3.9 g/cm³ vs limestone 2.7)

MINIMUM GRADE FOR SMELTING: 15% Cu (discard grey/white gangue)
REQUIRED EFFORT: 100 kg ore/worker/day (surface open-cast)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-D · Hematite / Magnetite (Iron Ore)

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Hematite: Fe₂O₃. Red-brown streak. Density 5.3 g/cm³. Mohs 5.5–6.5.
  Magnetite: Fe₃O₄. Black. Magnetic. Density 5.2 g/cm³.
  Fe content: hematite 70% Fe (theoretical), real ore 50–65%.

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  • Bahariya Oasis, Western Desert — 28.4°N 28.9°E
    Estimated reserve: 1 billion tonnes. Closest major source.
    Distance from Memphis: 365 km (12-day donkey caravan).
    Red-brown banded outcrops on plateau surface.
  • Aswan area — 24.1°N 32.9°E
    Banded ironstone in granite belt.
  • Sinai — trace deposits associated with copper belt.

HOW TO IDENTIFY:
  1. Hematite: red-brown streak on rough limestone chip.
     Reddish or metallic grey surface. Very dense.
  2. Magnetite: black, strongly attracted to any iron piece.
     (Carry a small iron sliver as a magnet test tool once you have it.)
  3. Both: density test — drop in water bucket alongside granite chip;
     iron ore sinks faster, clearly heavier per volume.

REQUIRED EFFORT: 100 kg ore/worker/day (surface collection)
  Underground mining (later, with iron tools): 40 kg/worker/day
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## R000-E · Natron (Sodium Carbonate)

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (hydrated) or Na₂CO₃ (anhydrous after drying).
  White, efflorescent salt crust on dry lake beds.
  Alkaline pH ~11.5 in solution. Reacts with fats (saponification),
  with acids (CO₂ fizz), with silica at high temperature (glass flux).

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  • Wadi Natrun, Western Desert — 30.4°N 30.2°E
    Distance from Memphis: 100 km (3-day walk, 2 days by donkey).
    Dry lake basin with thick white salt crust (natron beds).
    Harvesting: scrape surface crust with wooden paddle.
    Yield: 500 kg/worker/day during dry season (May–September).
  • Other dry lake playas in Western Desert (scattered).

HOW TO IDENTIFY:
  1. White efflorescent crust on dry lake floor.
  2. Strongly alkaline taste (do NOT swallow — just touch tongue).
  3. Dissolves in water (unlike gypsum which dissolves slowly).
  4. Fizzes vigorously with vinegar (CO₂ from carbonate).
  5. Solution turns red cabbage juice green (alkaline indicator).

GRADES:
  • Raw natron (field): ~70% Na₂CO₃, 20% NaCl, 10% Na₂SO₄
  • Purified (recrystallised from hot water): >95% Na₂CO₃

REQUIRED EFFORT: 500 kg raw natron/worker/day (surface scraping)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-F · Silica Sand (SiO₂)

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Quartz sand, >90% SiO₂. White to pale yellow grains.
  Grain size: 0.1–0.5 mm for glass-making.
  Melting point pure: 1,713°C (too high without flux).
  With natron flux: soda-lime glass melts at 1,000–1,200°C.

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  • White Desert, Western Egypt — abundant surface deposits
  • Sinai desert — very pure white quartz sand (30.5°N 34.2°E)
  • Wadi el-Arish deposits — Sinai
  • Dune sand (erg): collect from clean white dunes, not river
    silt (too much iron and clay contamination).

QUALITY TEST FOR GLASS USE:
  1. Colour: pure white (iron <0.1%) = good. Yellow tint = iron
     contamination → glass will be green.
  2. Rub between fingers: gritty (quartz) vs. smooth (clay) →
     clay contamination fails glass batch.
  3. Wash: pour sand in bucket of water, agitate, pour off
     top (clay and silt float off). Repeat 3×. Dry in sun.

REQUIRED EFFORT: 1 tonne/worker/day (surface collection + sieving)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-G · Refractory Clay (Kaolin-Rich)

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Clay with high kaolin (Al₂Si₂O₅(OH)₄) content.
  Fires to stoneware temperatures (1,200–1,350°C) without
  deforming. Essential for crucibles, kiln walls, tuyere pipes.

WHERE TO FIND IT:
  • Nile floodplain: abundant Nile silt clay — but fires to only
    ~900°C before softening. Inadequate for smelting crucibles.
  • BETTER: white/grey residual clay from limestone plateau edges,
    Eastern Desert wadis (28–26°N 33–32°E).
  • Best: kaolin outcrops near granite bodies (kaolinised granite),
    Aswan region (24.1°N 32.9°E).

FIELD TEST FOR QUALITY:
  1. Plastic test: roll into 3 mm coil. Bend coil 180°. If it
     bends without cracking: good plasticity = higher Al₂O₃.
  2. Shrinkage test: make 10 cm slab, dry, measure: >15% shrinkage
     = high clay (good quality, but add grog to prevent cracking).
  3. Fire test: make small test piece, fire over open charcoal
     fire. Good refractory clay remains hard and doesn't melt.
  4. Colour after firing: white/buff = high kaolin; red/brown
     = high iron (lower refractory quality).

REQUIRED EFFORT: 200 kg raw clay/worker/day (open pit digging)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## R000-H · Water

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS: H₂O. Liquid at 0–100°C (1 atm).
SOURCE: The Nile River. Available everywhere in Egypt.
  Nile flood: July–October (peak). Low water: April–June.
  Quality: turbid (silt-laden) in flood, clearer in dry season.

PURIFICATION (for drinking and sensitive processes):
  1. Settling: let stand 2 hours in clay pot → silt sinks.
  2. Filtration: pour through layers of sand + charcoal in
     a clay pot with a small hole in the base.
  3. Boiling: bring to rolling boil (100°C) for 2 minutes.
     Kills pathogens. Store in sealed clay vessel.

QUANTITIES PER PERSON PER DAY:
  • Drinking: 2–3 L minimum (more in desert/summer)
  • Heavy labour: 5–8 L
  • Pottery workshop: 50 L/day
  • Copper smelting team: 30 L/day (tapping + cooling)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## R000-I · Hardwood Timber

```
■ TERMINAL — raw earth (biological)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
WHAT IT IS:
  Dense, slow-grown wood. High lignin content. Required for tools,
  structures, charcoal, and fuel.

SPECIES AVAILABLE IN EGYPT:
  • Acacia nilotica (Nile acacia) — Nile banks throughout Egypt.
    Density: 750–900 kg/m³. Very hard. Good for tool handles,
    gear teeth, structural members.
  • Sycamore fig (Ficus sycomorus) — common, density ~450 kg/m³.
    Softer; good for large panels, furniture.
  • Tamarix (salt cedar) — wadi margins, desert edges.
    Density ~650 kg/m³. Tough, flexible. Good for spokes.
  • Cedar of Lebanon (Cedrus libani) — IMPORT REQUIRED.
    Source: Lebanon coastal mountains (33.9°N 35.5°E).
    Distance: 500 km by sea. Used for large structural beams,
    ship planking, furniture.

HARVESTING:
  • Use copper axe (later: bronze axe, later: iron axe).
  • Cut in dry season (November–March) — lower sap content.
  • Fell by cutting notch 1/3 depth on fall side, then back-cut.
  • Debark immediately: 10 blows with 2 kg stone maul loosens bark.

SEASONING (critical — green wood warps and cracks in structures):
  • Stack planks with 5 cm spacers (stickers) for air circulation.
  • Cover top against rain; leave sides open.
  • Minimum seasoning time:
    - 25 mm plank: 1 month per 25 mm = 1 month minimum
    - 50 mm timber: 2 months
    - 100 mm beam: 4 months
    - 150 mm hub stock: 6 months
  • Test for readiness: weigh, wait 1 week, weigh again.
    When weight stops changing: dry enough.

REQUIRED EFFORT: 20 kg felled + debarked timber/worker/day
  (without iron tools — copper axe)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```
